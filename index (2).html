How these were coded

I read every survey response. I used the pep-analyzer rules, and the file was read as text with no sampling. Each college has its own five named phases, and I only counted a phase when a direct quote from the student’s own words supported it. I checked every quote against the original text by script to confirm it was exact. When the evidence was ambiguous, I counted the phase as absent instead of guessing, so the method leans toward under-identifying. For every phase I did find, I also recorded whether the student did the work or only watched, and whether the work looked Low, Moderate or High in complexity, based only on the wording. Each response is then grouped by how many supported phases it shows: 3 or more phases, 2 phases, 1 phase, or no phases.

Common questions

What is this tab measuring?
Whether students’ own descriptions of their experience show the steps experts in their field go through, such as investigating, analyzing, designing, testing and communicating in engineering. Each college has five named phases that fit its kind of work. The tab counts how many of those phases each student’s written summary shows.

Why can I only see phases for one college at a time?
Each college’s phases have different names and meanings, so they can’t be added up or compared across colleges phase by phase. Choose a college in the filter to see its phases. Across colleges, compare the number of phases a response shows instead.
What do “3 or more phases”, “2 phases”, “1 phase” and “No phases” mean?
How many of the college’s five phases a single response shows evidence of. A response that shows 3 or more phases describes a fuller cycle of expert practice. 1 phase or none usually means the summary was brief or described one task.
What does “did the work or watched” mean?
For each phase found, whether the wording describes the student doing the work (Engaged) or watching others do it (Observed). When the wording doesn’t make the role clear, it is left out rather than guessed.
How is complexity judged?
From the wording only. High: the student describes iteration, trade-offs or sustained problem-solving. Moderate: some judgment or several steps. Low: routine or single-step work. When the text is too brief to judge, it isn’t rated.
Does a low number mean the experience was weak?
Not necessarily. The counts reflect what students wrote in a short summary, not everything they did. A student may have done all five phases and described only one. The method also counts a phase as absent whenever the evidence is unclear, so it leans toward under-counting.
How many responses are included?
13,509 responses: every response that has a written Student Summary.
Who did the reading, and how sure can we be?
Claude, an AI model, applied the pep-analyzer rules to every summary, and every supporting quote was checked against the original text by script. The figures are exact counts of what was found. A faculty coder should still check a sample before the numbers are used formally.
Why is my department’s number based on so few responses?
Some departments have few survey responses. When a selection is small, the page shows a warning: with fewer than 30 responses, one student can move a percentage by several points. Below 10 responses, percentages are hidden.
Can I see the student comments behind a number?
Not on this tab. To protect student privacy, no names or IDs are shown. The BYU Aims tab shows a few short example quotes, chosen so they don’t identify anyone.

How these were coded

Data. 13,514 responses. All three student-written answers are coded: Student Summary, Inspiring Learning, and the reasoning for the overall rating. Provider-written text (job description, title) is not.

How it was coded. Each answer is split into sentences, and a code is given only when a sentence matches that code’s phrase rules (for example, “Connection with God” needs God, the Lord, the Spirit, prayer or revelation named alongside an experience). The matching sentence is kept as the evidence. Spiritual codes are skipped in sentences that deny the experience (“I didn’t receive any revelation”). Nothing is inferred from the job title, field norms or what the student “probably” did.

How accurate it is. The rules were checked against 4,748 responses that AI readers had coded one by one under the same codebook. At the aim level they agree well: Spiritually Strengthening 87% precision / 77% recall, Character Building 84% / 74%, Intellectually Enlarging 76% / 86%, Life-Long Learning & Service 75% / 72%. Individual subcategories are less exact (most between 40% and 80%), so treat subcategory figures as direction, not precise counts. Phrase rules tend to miss things said in unusual words more than they invent things.

Patterns found in the comments. Recurring patterns found by reading the comments, beyond the slide’s subcategories, filed under the theme they fit. They are coded with the same phrase-rule method. “Plans for graduate or further education” needs grad school, a master’s, a PhD or similar named, so “I want to be a professor” alone does not count. “Intends to serve” holds the stated wishes to help that the slide’s “Served/helped others” leaves out. Across the page, a response counts toward an aim if it shows a slide subcategory or a pattern from the comments for that aim.

Other column. “Not inspired but describes inspiration” responses are also coded for whatever they go on to describe. “No answer or declined” covers answers like n/a, “not comfortable sharing” or blank marks.

Who coded. Phrase rules written and validated by Claude, an AI model. A faculty coder should check a subset before formal use. No names or IDs are on this page. The example quotes on each aim were chosen so they don’t identify anyone: each is short, contains no names, places or programs, and comes from a department with at least 100 responses. The coding sheet with all evidence sentences is kept separately.

Common questions

What is this tab measuring?
How often students’ own written answers describe each of the four aims of a BYU education: Intellectually Enlarging, Spiritually Strengthening, Character Building, and Life-Long Learning & Service. The subcategories under each aim come from the “Educational Aims of a BYU Education” inspiration themes, plus recurring patterns found by reading the comments.
Where do the numbers come from?
The program evaluation survey export: 13,514 unique responses from 15 colleges, 107 departments and 7 experience types, for the work terms in the export (from 2024 on). Three written answers are used for each response: the Student Summary, the “Inspiring Learning” comment, and the reasoning for the overall rating.
Was every response read? Is this a sample?
Every response is included; there is no sampling. Each answer was split into sentences, and phrase rules were applied to every sentence of every answer.
What does it mean that a response “shows” an aim?
At least one sentence in the student’s own words matches one of that aim’s subcategories. Nothing is inferred from the job title, the field, or what students in that program usually do. One response can show several aims, so the aim percentages add up to more than 100%.
How accurate is it?
The rules were checked against 4,748 responses that were read and coded one by one. For the four aims, the rules agree well: 75–87% of the codes they give are confirmed by the readers, and they find 72–86% of what the readers found. Individual subcategories are less exact (most between 40% and 80%). Use the figures to compare aims, colleges and programs, not as precise counts.
Does a low percentage mean students aren’t experiencing that aim?
No. It means fewer students wrote about it. The questions don’t ask about every aim, and students write briefly. A response that doesn’t mention an aim isn’t evidence that the aim was missing.
Why is Spiritually Strengthening lower than the other aims?
Spiritual content shows up almost entirely in the “Inspiring Learning” answer, which asks about inspiration or revelation directly (about 30% of responses there). The Student Summary and the rating reason rarely mention it (3–4%). The rules also require explicit religious wording, such as God, the Spirit, prayer or the gospel. Use the “Written answer” filter to see each question separately.
What does “Other” mean?
Responses that are too vague to code (“It was great”), that say they weren’t inspired, or that gave no usable answer. “Not inspired but describes inspiration” covers students who say nothing inspired them and then describe something that fits an aim; those are also counted under that aim.
Why is the total 13,514 here but 13,509 on the Patterns of Expert Practice tab?
That tab reads only the Student Summary, and 5 responses left it blank. This tab also uses the other two answers, so those 5 are included.
Why are only some subcategories shown in each aim?
Each aim box lists the subcategories that appear in at least 10% of that aim’s responses, to keep the view readable. The full set is in the coding spreadsheet.
Why is my department’s number based on so few responses?
Some departments and colleges have few survey responses. With fewer than 30, one student can move a percentage by several points, and the page shows a warning. Below 10 responses, percentages are hidden and the page says “too few to show.”
Can I see the comments behind a number?
Each aim shows two short example quotes, drawn from all colleges and shown without names or programs, chosen so they don’t identify anyone. No names or IDs are on this page. A separate coding spreadsheet lists, for every code, the exact sentence it came from, so any number can be traced to the students’ words by the people who hold that file.
Who did the coding? Was a person involved?
Claude, an AI model, wrote and tested the rules, and the results were checked against responses read individually. A faculty member should review a sample of the coded sentences before the numbers are used in formal decisions.
